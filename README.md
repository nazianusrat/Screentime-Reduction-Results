# Screentime-Reduction-Results
There are three main reasons the overall evidence looks weaker than any single successful trial might suggest. First, many studies rely on self-reported screen time, which participants may report inaccurately, especially when they know what the study expects; studies that use objective data, such as logged phone-usage records, usually find smaller effects or none at all (Zimmermann, 2021; Castelo et al., 2025). Second, many studies draw on similar samples, largely university students, often from a single country and studied over a short period (Kasturiratna et al., 2025; Zimmermann, 2021) who tend to be more motivated and self-aware than the general population, especially compared with people who struggle most with excessive smartphone use, which limits generalizability (Precht et al., 2023; Dekker et al., 2024; Loid et al., 2020). Third, publication bias likely inflates the apparent effectiveness of these interventions, since studies that find no effect are less likely to be published (Žmavc et al., 2025; Radtke et al., 2022)
